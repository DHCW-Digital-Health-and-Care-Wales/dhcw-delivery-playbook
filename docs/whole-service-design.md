# Designing the whole service

A service is everything a person goes through to get something done. Our software is usually one part of that. Around it sit letters, phone calls, text messages, reception desks, clinics, and the steps people take before and after they ever see a screen. If we design the screen well but ignore the rest, the person still has a bad time.

This chapter is about designing for the whole journey, including the parts that sit outside our software.

## Solve the whole problem

People don't experience organisational boundaries. A parent booking a child's vaccination doesn't know or care which team owns which system, or where DHCW ends and a health board begins. They experience one journey, and they judge it as a whole.

So a team has to look up from its own backlog and ask what the person is really trying to do, from start to finish. The real goal sits behind the task. A parent booking a vaccination is trying to protect their child. When we design for that deeper goal, we often find the most useful improvement sits just outside our system, in a confusing letter or a step that makes people phone up.

The **Service Designer** role (see [Team roles](team-roles.md)) leads this thinking. Their job is to map the end-to-end journey across every touchpoint and team boundary, and to find where a change will do the most good. A whole team benefits from thinking this way, whatever people's roles.

## Map the journey

You can't design a whole service you can't see. Journey mapping makes the whole thing visible. You lay out every step a person takes, in order, and mark what they're doing, what they're thinking, and where it goes wrong.

A useful service map at DHCW shows:

- Every step, digital and non-digital, from the person's first thought to the final outcome.
- Who and what is involved at each step, including other teams, systems, and organisations.
- The moments that matter most, where people get stuck, drop out, or need help.
- The gaps and handoffs between channels, which is usually where journeys break.

Use a real journey to do this: a child's immunisation from invitation to record, say, or a pharmacy consultation from walk-in to follow-up. Real journeys surface real problems that a made-up example would smooth over. The [UCD playbooks](https://nhswales365.sharepoint.com/sites/DHC_UCD/SitePages/UCD-Playbooks.aspx) cover mapping methods in more detail (NHS Wales sign-in required).

## Join up the channels

Most health journeys run through more than one channel. A letter invites someone. A website lets them book. A text reminds them. A clinician sees them. A record updates. If those channels don't tell a consistent story, people fall through the cracks between them.

Joining up means:

- The information a person gets is consistent across letter, text, web, and in person.
- What someone does in one channel is known in the next, so they don't have to start again or repeat themselves.
- People who can't or won't use the digital route can still complete the journey another way, and that route is designed with the same care.

Point 4 of the Digital Service Standard for Wales (provide a joined up experience) and our own duty to serve everyone in Wales both point the same way. A digital service that only works for digital-confident people isn't finished.

## Keep the whole thing simple

Simple to use is a property of the whole journey. A clean screen doesn't help if the letter that led to it was baffling, or if finishing the task means a phone call the person didn't expect.

Keeping a service simple means removing steps people don't need, using plain language everywhere (see the Content Designer role in [Team roles](team-roles.md)), and testing the whole flow with real users rather than checking each screen on its own. When a person can get from their first thought to a finished outcome without confusion, extra help, or an unwanted phone call, the service is doing its job.
